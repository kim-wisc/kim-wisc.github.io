Seven short reviews of the statics that Mechanics of Materials builds on, in the order the course needs them. Conventions used throughout (as in the lecture notes): x to the right, y up, z toward you; forces positive along +x, +y; moments and couples positive counter-clockwise (+z, right-hand rule); internal axial force drawn in tension so that tension comes out positive. Each tab ends with a Check yourself box built from the course's own examples.

Why it matters: every problem in this course starts with a free-body diagram (M01), and whether a beam, bar or shaft is statically determinate decides whether statics alone is enough (M03, M11). Each support type removes certain motions and therefore supplies the matching reaction components.

Presets (M11):

Reaction components

Rule of thumb for a planar body: 3 independent equilibrium equations (ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0). Fewer than 3 unknown reactions → the body can move (a mechanism); exactly 3, properly arranged → statically determinate; more than 3 → statically indeterminate, and you will need deformation (compatibility) equations from M02–M03 and M11 in addition to statics. Reactions that are all parallel or all concurrent are improperly arranged even if there are three of them.

Why it matters: the first examples of the course (M01, M02, M03, M05) are rigid bars held by pins and links. You need to (1) pick a moment center that eliminates unknowns, (2) recognize two-force members, whose force acts along the line joining their pins, and (3) resolve that force into components from the geometry.

Bracket (M01) bar AB pinned at A, link BC to the wall at C

Take moments about:

Two-force member force along the line of the pins

Moment of a force about a point: MO = ±(force) × (perpendicular distance), counter-clockwise positive; or add the moments of the components, each with its own arm. A good moment center is one through which as many unknown forces as possible pass, so they drop out.

Why it matters: stresses come from internal forces — the axial force N (M01–M03), the torque T (M06), and the shear and moment V, M (M07–M08). All are found the same way: cut the member, keep one side, draw the unknown internal resultant in its positive direction, and apply equilibrium to the piece you kept.

Why it matters: in combined loading (M10) a force applied away from the section produces, at the cut, an axial force, two shears, a torque and two bending moments. The tool is the moment of a force about a point in 3-D, M = r × F, taken component by component with the right-hand rule (M10).

Quick sets:

At the section A (the wall) equal and opposite to the resultant of the applied load about A

Internal resultants at A: N = Fx (axial, along the bar), Vy = Fy, Vz = Fz, T = Mx (torque, about the bar axis), My and Mz (bending). The sign tells you which side of the section is in tension: with σx = −Mzy/I, a positive Mz puts the −y (bottom) fibers in tension and the +y fibers in compression; likewise for My and z. Deciding zero / non-zero and + / − for each term at each point is exactly the exercise of M10.

Why it matters: most wrong answers in this course are unit slips: mm vs m, kN vs N, psi vs ksi, degrees instead of radians in φ = TL/(GJ) (M06), or 5.623×10⁸ Pa where 562 MPa is expected (M01).

Consistent sets

SI: N, m, Pa (N/m²) — or N, mm, MPa (N/mm²): 1 MPa = 1 N/mm² exactly, so forces in N and lengths in mm give stresses in MPa directly.
US: lb, in, psi — or kip, in, ksi: 1 ksi = 1 kip/in².
Angles in radians wherever they multiply or divide a length (arc length, twist, curvature, shear strain γ).
